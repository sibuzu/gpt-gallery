High-Angle Figure Construction: Drawing "Looking Down" Poses From Reference 📸
 
A high-angle photo (like someone shooting down at their subject) creates dramatic foreshortening that trips up a lot of artists. Here's how this sheet breaks down the process:
 
1️⃣ First one use eye-angle photo. Identify your landmark sections from the reference(first left quarter box). Head, shoulders, ribcage, pelvis, thighs, knees, and calves — mark where each of these falls in your reference photo before drawing a single construction line. 
 
2️⃣ Build the basic construction first. Block in the figure using simple shapes, paying close attention to how the high angle compresses the torso and elongates the perspective toward the feet.
 
3️⃣ Refine with center line and tilt. Add the center line, note the tilt of the body, and mark the shoulder line, ribcage, and pelvis — these should all reflect the specific angle of your reference photo.
 
4️⃣ Account for foreshortening throughout. A high-angle "looking down" view compresses the upper body while the lower body (closer to camera in this framing) may appear larger — track this perspective shift carefully.
 
5️⃣ Add form, detail, volumes, and contours. Once construction is solid, layer in anatomy, clothing, and shadow — this is where the reference photo's specific styling (hair, outfit, accessories) comes through.
 
6️⃣ Apply the same method to a different high-angle pose. A selfie-style pose tests your construction system against a completely different gesture, still within the same high-angle perspective challenge.
 
7️⃣ Use a perspective box to maintain proportions. Building a rough 3D box around the figure keeps your proportions honest even as the pose and angle become more complex.
 
8️⃣ Try the same pose from the back. Constructing the identical pose from behind reinforces that your understanding of the 3D form holds up from any viewing angle, not just the one in your reference.
 
9️⃣ Break the whole figure into 4 core volumes. Head as a sphere, ribcage as a box, pelvis as a box, and legs as cylinders — this simplification is your fallback for any pose, at any angle.
 
🔟 Use a character reference for proportion and design ideas. Studying professional character designs alongside your own construction work helps calibrate proportion choices and stylistic details.
